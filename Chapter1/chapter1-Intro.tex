\chapter{Introduction to Digitisation of Historical Inscriptions and Manuscripts}
This chapter frames the digitisation challenge for South Asian historical inscriptions and manuscripts and situates the project within heritage preservation and modern AI-assisted workflows. It introduces the project title and context, then moves into the motivation, problem statement, and objectives that drive the work. A brief methodology overview outlines the staged pipeline from preprocessing, enhancement, and binarisation to a design provision for OCR routing, and finally structured record assembly. The section on assumptions and constraints clarifies the operational boundaries, and the chapter closes by summarizing how the rest of the report is organized.

\section[Introduction]{Introduction}
South Asia preserves a vast body of historical knowledge recorded across various physical mediums including stone inscriptions, palm leaf manuscripts, copper plates, paper manuscripts, cave or rock paintings, and direct paper rubbings (estampages). Many of these artefacts are fragile, geographically dispersed, and visually degraded, which makes manual transcription slow and dependent on scarce specialist expertise. While major archives provide scanned images, they rarely include a reliable digitisation and binarisation layer that allows the material to be searched, compared, or cited in a structured form.

This project, titled \textit{Digitisation of Historical Inscriptions and Manuscripts}, addresses that gap through a unified, six-artefact digitisation pipeline. The workflow takes degraded scans and produces an enhanced and binarised image, calculates ECE signal processing quality metrics, and generates a structured metadata record that can be exported as JSON or a researcher-ready PDF. Unlike single-medium systems, the design routes each of the six artefact types through tailored enhancement and binarisation steps to preserve stroke legibility and material context. While a baseline design provision for Optical Character Recognition (OCR) routing is laid out as Stage 4 of the pipeline, full-scale character transcription is treated as an extension, with the current phase focusing on ensuring clean, high-contrast binarised outputs.

The work presented in this report focuses on Phase 1 of the pipeline: preprocessing, signal enhancement, binarisation, and structured record assembly. This section sets the technical and historical context for the project and anchors the motivation, problem statement, and objectives that follow.

\section[Literature Review]{Literature Review}
This review focuses on recent work in historical document enhancement, binarisation, OCR, layout analysis, and translation, with an emphasis on studies most relevant to South Asian inscriptions and manuscripts.

To facilitate general computer vision tasks, Bradski outlines the OpenCV library, which provides a comprehensive suite of real-time image processing utilities essential for core preprocessing steps like filtering, morphology, and colour conversions \cite{OpenCV2000}. For specialized colour restoration, Harman introduces DStretch for rock art enhancement through color decorrelation, presenting a highly effective visual method that is not paired with OCR or structured text extraction \cite{Harman2008}. To reconstruct fine details in degraded images, Wang et al. propose Real-ESRGAN for blind super-resolution on real degraded photos, demonstrating strong perceptual restoration, but the evaluation is not conducted on inscription imagery or document-specific degradations \cite{Wang2021}. For denoising, Pramanik et al. apply deep learning to degraded handwritten documents, achieving clear noise suppression, yet the experiments are centered on European scripts rather than Indic historical sources \cite{Pramanik2021}.

In the domain of image binarisation, Otsu introduces a classic global thresholding method based on grey-level histograms to maximize class variance, providing a baseline for document image binarisation under uniform lighting conditions \cite{Otsu1979}. Addressing uneven exposure and degradation, Sauvola and Pietik{\"a}inen propose an adaptive thresholding algorithm that dynamically adjusts the local threshold using mean and standard deviation, which has become a foundational standard for historical document binarisation \cite{Sauvola2000}. For palm leaf manuscripts, Dhanya et al. release the THPLMD dataset and apply Sauvola adaptive thresholding for Tamil palm leaf binarisation, improving foreground extraction but stopping short of transcription or translation stages \cite{Dhanya2020}.

For layout analysis and dataset construction, Mehrotra et al. introduce Indiscapes for layout analysis of Indic manuscripts using CNN-based segmentation, yet the scope is limited to page layout and does not address enhancement, binarisation, or transcription quality \cite{Mehrotra2022}. Seuret et al. introduce the DIVA-HisDB dataset as a precisely annotated large-scale benchmark for layout analysis and text transcription of historical handwriting and medieval scripts \cite{Seuret2016}, while they also propose the corresponding DIVA-HisDB framework for historical document analysis with layout and transcription components, though the focus is European medieval manuscripts without Indic script coverage \cite{Seuret2021}. Additionally, Burie et al. report on the ICFHR 2016 competition on palm leaf manuscripts, benchmarking binarisation methods and highlighting the difficulty of noise and texture, but without any AI enhancement or end-to-end transcription \cite{Burie2016}.

To support these classification and segmentation tasks, deep neural network architectures have evolved significantly. LeCun, Bengio, and Hinton provide a comprehensive review of deep learning, explaining the core principles of backpropagation, convolutional networks, and recurrent networks in signal reconstruction and pattern recognition \cite{LeCun2015}. For training optimization, Kingma and Ba introduce the Adam optimization algorithm, which uses adaptive estimates of lower-order moments to optimize gradient descent steps during deep learning model training \cite{Adam2015}. In terms of feature extraction, Simonyan and Zisserman evaluate deep convolutional networks for large-scale image recognition, showing that very deep architectures like VGG-16 can learn robust representations for downstream vision tasks \cite{VGG2015}. To improve deep model stability, He et al. propose Deep Residual Learning (ResNet) to address the vanishing gradient problem, enabling stable training of architectures exceeding one hundred layers \cite{ResNet2016}. For pixel-level segmentation, Ronneberger et al. developed the U-Net architecture for biomedical image segmentation, which uses a contracting path and an expansive path with skip connections to preserve high-resolution spatial features \cite{UNet2015}. Tensmeyer and Martinez present a document image binarisation approach using fully convolutional networks (FCNs), demonstrating the effectiveness of deep learning models in learning spatial context without manual feature tuning \cite{Tensmeyer2017}. Beyond convolutional models, Vaswani et al. proposed the Transformer architecture based entirely on attention mechanisms, which serves as the foundational backbone for modern sequence translation and sequence-to-sequence learning \cite{Vaswani2017}. Similarly, Dosovitskiy et al. demonstrate that the Transformer architecture can be applied directly to image patches for visual recognition at scale, challenging traditional convolutional neural networks in image classification tasks \cite{ViT2021}.

For traditional machine learning and object detection, Ho introduces Random Decision Forests, an ensemble classifier that constructs multiple decision trees during training to achieve high classification accuracy and robustness against noise \cite{Ho1995}. Viola and Jones propose a rapid object detection framework based on Haar-like features and AdaBoost, showing that simple, cascaded classifiers can achieve real-time performance on complex visual search tasks \cite{Viola2001}. For faster deep learning-based localization, Girshick introduces the Fast R-CNN framework for fast object detection, utilizing a Region of Interest pooling layer to accelerate bounding box classification and coordinate regression \cite{FastRCNN2015}.

In the field of character recognition and OCR pipelines, Smith presents the architectural design of the Tesseract Optical Character Recognition engine, describing its line-finding, character-grouping, and linguistic analysis modules which serve as a primary open-source OCR option \cite{Smith2007}. The EasyOCR framework by JaidedAI leverages deep learning models for character recognition across multiple languages, providing a user-friendly and robust alternative to traditional template-matching OCR systems \cite{EasyOCR2020}. For sequence modeling, Graves presents supervised sequence labelling techniques using recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and connectionist temporal classification (CTC), which are crucial for transcribing unsegmented text lines \cite{Graves2012}. Furthermore, Breuel details a high-performance OCR pipeline for printed English and Fraktur using long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, proving the superiority of recurrent models over traditional character-level classifiers \cite{Breuel2013}. Aitha et al. present a Brahmi character dataset and a custom CNN for Ashokan Brahmi, which is a critical step toward OCR for ancient scripts but remains an early-stage dataset effort without integration into a full processing pipeline \cite{Aitha2023}. Krishnan et al. use deep CNNs for OCR of Tamil and Devanagari, providing script-specific recognition but not incorporating ensemble strategies or confidence-driven fusion \cite{Krishnan2019}. Valy et al. build a deep learning pipeline for Khmer palm leaf manuscripts using CNN and LSTM for OCR, but the approach is language-specific and is not validated on South Asian scripts or inscription media \cite{Valy2021}.

For post-OCR linguistic analysis and translation, AI4Bharat's Bhashini initiative provides transformer-based NLP resources for Indian languages, but its focus is modern language usage and it provides limited support for classical or epigraphic forms \cite{AI4Bharat2022}. The IndicNLP library provides specialized natural language processing utilities for Indian languages, enabling tokenization, script conversion, and linguistic analysis necessary for South Asian historical texts \cite{IndicNLP2022}. For multilingual translation, Helsinki-NLP's OPUS-MT models enable transformer-based translation for Dravidian and Indic languages, yet performance degrades on archaic or classical text varieties common in inscriptions \cite{OPUSMT2020}.

To contextualize these technologies within historical studies, Roy, Pal, and Llad{\'o}s present a comprehensive survey of ancient document recognition and historical manuscript transcription, highlighting key challenges in binarisation, layout analysis, and script identification \cite{Roy2020}. Uchida surveys image processing and recognition techniques for historical documents, summarizing classical layout analysis, stroke extraction, and character classification methodologies \cite{Uchida2013}. From a preservation standpoint, the UNESCO Memory of the World Programme provides guidelines and standards for documentary heritage preservation, emphasizing the role of digital archives in safeguarding endangered historical assets \cite{UNESCO2023}.

Finally, to support software engineering and deployment of these pipelines, modern development frameworks are utilized. The TensorFlow development team provides an end-to-end open-source machine learning platform that facilitates the training and inference of deep neural networks for binarisation and segmentation \cite{TensorFlow2024}. The FastAPI documentation outlines a high-performance web framework for building APIs with Python, enabling rapid and asynchronous integration between backend processing pipelines and client interfaces \cite{FastAPI2024}. The React documentation details a component-based frontend library for building dynamic and interactive user interfaces, allowing researchers to visualize original and processed manuscript comparisons \cite{React2024}.

Collectively, these studies show strong individual advances in enhancement, binarisation, OCR, layout analysis, and translation, but also reveal a gap in integrated, multi-artefact pipelines tuned to South Asian epigraphy and manuscript collections.

\section[Motivation]{Motivation}
Preserving inscriptions and manuscripts is essential for historical scholarship, yet the materials are often degraded, fragile, and dispersed across archives, limiting access and slowing research. Manual transcription is time-consuming and depends on scarce expertise in ancient scripts, while existing digital collections typically provide only scanned images without searchable text. Recent advances in image enhancement and OCR make it feasible to recover faint characters and create reliable digital records. This project is motivated by the need for an end-to-end, repeatable pipeline that improves legibility, extracts Unicode text, and produces citable records to accelerate research and preservation.

\section[Problem statement]{Problem statement}
Historical inscriptions and manuscripts in South Asia remain largely inaccessible because their scans are visually degraded and lack accurate, machine-readable transcriptions. Current archival workflows provide images but do not offer a consistent, automated path from raw scans to enhanced images, verified OCR output, and structured metadata suitable for search and citation. The problem is to design and implement a reliable, multi-artefact digitisation pipeline that can enhance degraded images, binarise text regions, extract script-accurate Unicode transcription, and assemble preservation-ready records while respecting non-destructive processing constraints.

\section[Objectives]{Objectives}
\begin{enumerate}
\item To develop a non-destructive, six-artefact digitisation pipeline for stone inscriptions, palm leaf manuscripts, copper plates, paper manuscripts, cave/rock paintings, and direct paper rubbings (estampages), with preprocessing and enhancement for improved legibility.
\item To achieve robust document binarisation to extract clean, high-contrast foreground text strokes from complex background substrates, while establishing a baseline design and routing provision for script-aware OCR ensemble processing.
\item To generate structured, citable digital records by combining original/processed images, ECE quality metrics, and artefact metadata in exportable JSON and researcher-ready PDF formats.
\end{enumerate}

\section[Brief Methodology of the project]{Brief Methodology of the project}
The project follows an end-to-end staged methodology designed for historical image degradation and substrate diversity, consisting of the following sequential pipeline:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Stage 1: Preprocessing (Signal Correction): Raw scans are normalised by correcting EXIF orientation, applying CLAHE in the CIELAB luminance space to address uneven exposure, performing Grey-World auto white balance, and removing margins via morphological border cropping.
    \item Stage 2: Signal Enhancement (Reconstruction): Background speckle and noise are filtered using Non-Local Means (NLM) denoising. Resolution is upscaled using Real-ESRGAN x2 super-resolution, with DStretch colour decorrelation stretch applied to faded palm leaves and cave paintings to enhance pigment variance. Fused unsharp masking boosts edge gradients.
    \item Stage 3: Binarisation (Feature Extraction): Documents are automatically classified to route them to tailored thresholding pipelines, including adaptive Sauvola binarisation with dynamic parameter tuning, character-level local segmentation for palm leaves, Otsu global binarisation for paper/rubbings, and copper plate rectangular mask filtering. Deep learning (LightUNet and DocEnTr patch-ViT) options are provided with a binary entropy fallback.
    \item Stage 4: OCR (Architectural Provision): The base architectural design and layout routing framework are established as a provision for OCR processing, though actual text recognition and transcription are not completed in this implementation and remain a future extension.
\end{itemize}

\section[Assumptions made / Constraints of the project]{Assumptions made / Constraints of the project}
The validity of this project is based on the following assumptions and constraints:
\begin{itemize}
\item Input artefacts are already available as scanned or photographed 2D images in standard formats (JPEG/PNG/TIFF), and physical hardware acquisition or field scanning is outside the project scope.
\item Original archival scans are treated as read-only assets; all pipeline stages are strictly non-destructive and write intermediate and final outputs to dedicated, isolated directories.
\item The active implementation covers preprocessing, enhancement, binarisation, quality metrics calculation, and record assembly; transcription models for ancient scripts like Brahmi and Grantha are not production-ready and require manual verification, which keeps the current OCR routing phase as a design provision only.
\item As an empirical guideline from current project observations, images with more than 50\% surface damage (for example, severe erosion, missing surface regions, or heavy staining) often fail standard binarisation thresholds and require manual intervention.
\item Compute and performance benchmarks are based on a quad-core workstation environment where CPU-bound processing (e.g., Real-ESRGAN upscaling) remains the primary execution bottleneck, and CUDA acceleration is required to achieve real-time throughput.
\item Operation costs scale with the deployment architecture; local GPU-enabled workstations incur zero operational cost for active digitisation sites, while cloud deployment (such as AWS g4dn.xlarge with S3) is reserved for public archival search and access.
\end{itemize}

\section[Organization of the report]{Organization of the report}
This report is organized as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item Chapter 2 presents the foundational concepts for digital heritage preservation, including degradation mechanisms across the six artefact categories, image processing fundamentals (CLAHE, white balance, Real-ESRGAN, DStretch), binarisation techniques, OCR principles, custom frequency/spatial filters, and self-reference quality assessment metrics.
\item Chapter 3 details the design of the proposed AI-assisted digitisation pipeline, including the stage-wise sequential architecture, mathematical formulations (CLAHE, Grey-World, Sauvola tuning, Gabor, Sobel, FFT notch filters), and the baseline OCR routing design.
\item Chapter 4 explains the implementation of the pipeline, focusing on the software implementation of preprocessing, signal enhancement, specialized binarisation routing, the custom filters, and the React/FastAPI web interface.
\item Chapter 5 reports the experimental results and discussions, covering self-reference quality metrics (PSNR, SSIM, CNR, ink coverage checks), color channel statistical distributions (skewness and kurtosis), algorithm suitability analysis, and processing throughput benchmarks.
\item Chapter 6 concludes the work by summarizing objective-wise outcomes, limitations of the present binarisation/enhancement phase, and future scope including the completion of the OCR transcription engine and translation modules.
\end{itemize}
